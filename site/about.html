How it works

A prototype that asks one question: can museums' open data be joined well enough to search as one collection? It takes paintings made 1850–1900 from three museums, and everything five museums hold by Rembrandt, joins them in a knowledge graph through Wikidata, and builds a search and an AI question box on top.

The pipeline

  1. Harvest from open APIs. The Rijksmuseum's linked data service, The Met Collection API and the Art Institute of Chicago API, and for Rembrandt also SMK (the National Gallery of Denmark) and the Cleveland Museum of Art. Requests are sequential and spaced out, every response is cached, and the project never writes to any museum.
  2. Convert to Linked Art. Each record becomes Linked Art JSON-LD with its provenance: source, URL, fetch date and licence, and a separate licence on any field that differs.
  3. Match every artist to Wikidata. A museum's direct Wikidata link counts. Otherwise, two independent IDs must agree (for example AIC's artist ID and ULAN), or one ID plus the same birth and death years. Everything else goes to a review list rather than being merged. Each artist page shows how its records were matched. SMK and Cleveland give only their own artist IDs, which Wikidata doesn't list, so their artists match on name and life years and wait for a person to confirm each museum's artist once.
  4. Resolve concepts. Styles and subjects (AAT terms from the Met and AIC, Iconclass codes from the Rijksmuseum) are resolved to Wikidata items. A few links the project asserts itself, such as landscape (AAT) ≈ landscape (Iconclass), sit in a separate graph.
  5. Build the knowledge graph. Everything is loaded into Oxigraph as RDF, with a named graph per source, one for project alignments and one for inferences. An artist's movement on Wikidata becomes an inferred style, never the museum's claim. A print held by several museums is one composition printed many times: impressions citing the same catalogue number (Bartsch or New Hollstein) are grouped under one print, never by title alone.
  6. Build the search from the graph. SPARQL queries over the graph produce one document per work, indexed with MiniSearch. The search runs entirely in your browser.
  7. Publish as a static site. No server: the search index, the graph (run in the browser as WebAssembly) and each work's Linked Art file are static files. Only “Ask the graph” uses a small proxy, to call the AI model without exposing a key.

Search or Ask?

The two boxes look alike but do different jobs, and each shows off a different way of working with a knowledge graph.

SearchAsk the graph
You typeWords: “snow”, “Impressionist landscapes”, “Monet”A question: “Which artists have paintings in all three museums?”
You getWorks to look at, ranked and grouped by artist, with imagesAn answer: a table, and one sentence checked against it
HowA search index built from the graph, in your browserClaude writes one SPARQL query; the graph runs it in your browser
AIOnly the AI-suggested subjects, which you can switch offWrites the query and the sentence, never the numbers
Good atFinding and browsing; typos, synonyms and related conceptsCounting, comparing and joining facts across museums

Use Search to find things. It is instant, free and forgiving, and says why each result matched. It shows the classic knowledge-graph-to-search workflow: records from different museums reconciled to shared Wikidata artists and concepts, then flattened into one search document per work, so one query reaches every museum and each artist's works stay together.

Use Ask for an answer no single record holds: “how many”, “in all three museums”, “born in”, “the AI tagged but the museum didn't”. Those need joins across the graph that keyword search can't do. It shows a grounded AI workflow: plain English turned into a query you can read and edit, a guard that allows only read-only queries, an answer sentence whose every number must appear in the results, and evaluations (19 of 20 paintings questions and 7 of 7 Rembrandt questions exactly right) re-run whenever the instructions change.

Use Explore to write SPARQL yourself, or to adjust a query Ask wrote.

What it found

The test question was whether “Impressionist landscapes” returns works from all three museums, grouped correctly by artist. So far it returns AIC and the Met, but not the Rijksmuseum, and grouping is correct where an artist appears in several museums.

  • AIC states the style itself, so its Impressionist landscapes match on its own data.
  • The Met has no style field; its works match only through the artist's movement on Wikidata.
  • The Rijksmuseum's painters of the period are mostly the Hague School, which Wikidata keeps apart from Impressionism. The site offers it as a related movement rather than merging the two.

The full reports are in the repository: Impressionist landscapes and the earlier single-artist study, Van Gogh across three museums.

AI in this project

The museums record subjects unevenly: one museum tags snow, another doesn't tag anything at all. An AI model fills that gap, and an evaluation measures whether it helped.

  • Subjects from images. Claude Opus 5.5 looks at each openly licensed image and suggests what it depicts (snow, boats, a portrait of a woman), choosing only from a fixed list of concepts with verified Wikidata entries, so it cannot invent identifiers. It never suggests a style or movement: those come from the museums and Wikidata.
  • Labelled and separate. Every suggestion keeps its confidence and a short note of what the model saw, in its own named graph (graph/ai). Only suggestions at 60% confidence or more reach search, and they count for less than the museums' own subjects. Results found only through them carry an “AI” mark, and the search page can switch them off.
  • Measured. Twenty test searches are scored with and without AI suggestions. A second model, Claude Sonnet 5.5, judges whether each result is relevant from the image and catalogue data, so the model is not grading its own work. The evaluation report has the numbers.
  • Review assist. For each artist match too weak to merge automatically, Claude proposes a decision and cites the evidence; a person accepts or rejects it. Artist pages show “AI proposed, a person approved”.
  • Ask the graph. Claude turns a question into SPARQL; the graph answers it. See Search or Ask?
